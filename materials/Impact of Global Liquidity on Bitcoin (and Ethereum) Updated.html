Adding Experience of Past 6 Months To Our Original Estimates
Michael Howell
Oct 08, 2025

The Global Liquidity Background

Global Liquidity is having one of its periodic slowdowns, but overall the upward trend remains intact. The chart below confirms how the 3-month absolute change has dropped back, although it remains in positive territory. Alongside we report our in-house index of market liquidity. This measures the ability of investors to transact in size, close to current prices. Typically, it follows and corroborates the message from the Global Liquidity funding measure.

What’s more, markets demonstrate few of the ‘normal’ warning signs of a coming peak. In our experience, these come under six heads. All reduce Global Liquidity:

  • Monetary tightening – both from higher policy rates and from volatility in collateral markets

  • Breakdown in the monetary plumbing – dislocations between debt and liquidity, typically emanating from the repo markets and shadow banking

  • Soaring oil prices – oil is a heavy user of liquidity and can drain financial markets as well as stoking inflation

  • Strong real economy – similar, but more general argument. Inflation risk

  • Strong US dollar – reduces cross-border credit growth and pressurizes debtors

  • Sky high investor sentiment – when the ‘last’ investor has bought, the market becomes saturated and must fall

Of these warnings, only the first two currently have any merit. We know the supply of previously ’termed out’ debt is slated to soon need refinancing. What’s more, Fed Liquidity is slated to slow markedly over the next six months. Prudence also says that prevailing low volatility can only increase and when 80% of World Central Banks are easing, things cannot get much better! Yet, alongside, oil prices are flat-lining; the World real economy is sluggish; the US dollar still looks weakish, certainly relative to gold, and standard measures of investor sentiment sit only slightly above average.

The Bitcoin Cocktail

Before digging deeper into the Global Liquidity question, let’s dwell on the collective impact of the gold, liquidity and investor sentiment cocktail. It is important because Bitcoin is extremely sensitive to all three factors. The following pie chart shows our updated statistical results. This derives from an analysis run over the 2017-2025 period, using weekly data. We can identify four systematic influences on Bitcoin. Admittedly, Global Liquidity is the strongest and most significant of the four, but investor sentiment and gold also affect BTC$. Park that thought: we shall return later.

Tracing The Impact Of Global Liquidity Shocks

From a VAR (Vector Autoregression) framework we can identify the impact on BTC$ prices of a 1% point one-off shock (roughly 1sd) to Global Liquidity. The impulse response is plotted below. This chart records the deviation in the Bitcoin price. The take-away is that the impact starts slowly, builds up to a peak of 2% some three-months after the initial shock and then dies away.

Evidence below the actual experience in practice, using weekly data since 2022. Based on this levels chart BTC$ currently looks well-supported. Note we have shifted the Global Liquidity (GLI$) data (orange line) forwards by 12 weeks.

Of course, trending data can mislead us by generating spurious correlations. Hence, we can better test the robustness of this relationship by plotting 6-week changes in the variables (to eliminate ‘noise’). See the next chart. Visually, the average three-month ahead lead-time appears to hold up well.

We accept the criticism that the latest liquidity spike appears to have passed Bitcoin by. Does that mean the BTC$ relationship to liquidity is starting to break down? Not necessarily, because many other things are simultaneously going on. Not only are the other two key influences (i.e. gold and investor sentiment), cited above, also changing, but there is competition for funds coming from rival cryptocurrencies. Evidence the relationship between 6-week changes in Global Liquidity (GLI$) and Ethereum prices in the next chart. ETH$ has lately responded exactly in-line with prior expectations. Note how the lead-times between BTC$ and ETH$ versus Global Liquidity differ by some 3-4 weeks.

Impact of Global Liquidity on Other Assets

Overall, we must acknowledge that rising Global Liquidity can potentially float many boats. Therefore, the sensitivities of various assets to Global Liquidity shocks will not only differ cross-sectionally, but they will also vary over time. Evidence the following updated estimates:

Reassuringly, for several assets these loadings (estimated from 2015 to October 2025 using weekly data) appear robust over time. For example, gold, Bitcoin, Ethereum, Solana and several international stock markets. Others, namely for silver, NASDAQ, the yield curve (YC) and commodities have changed.

An obvious question from the keen-eyed is why the sensitivity reported above between BTC$ and Global Liquidity (circa 8x) looks very different from the 2x multiplier reported earlier in the impulse response chart? In short, the 8x estimate is not as good. First, it is derived from a simple bivariate regression using historic data. This showed what happened over a specific time period: it is backward not forward-looking. Second, this analysis does not allow for the impact of other factors, such as rising or falling gold and changing investor sentiment, which will affect BTC$. The earlier VAR estimate attempts to adjust for both distortions. Therefore, although the loadings are lower we should probably put more confidence in this result.

Using a VAR also allows us to better understand some of the complex interactions between Global Liquidity, Bitcoin and other assets and influences. Consider how the VAR model is ‘wired’ from this network diagram of key influences:

We have included the S&P500 index in this network to show its connections. Note that stock prices do not feature in the original pie chart. This is simply because their effect is already captured by the investor sentiment term. Here, we are modelling sequential changes. Note also that the bold arrows are statistically significant at the 5% level and the shaded arrows at the 10% level (i.e. less persuasive).

The strongest effects on Bitcoin again come from Global Liquidity, which also impacts gold and the S&P500. Risk appetite also affects Bitcoin, as noted, through a feedback effect whereby strong Bitcoin gains further improve sentiment. This two-way interaction is also seen between Bitcoin and the S&P500 and between gold and Bitcoin. Both are important.

Watching Feedback Effects

The linkage between the S&P500 and Bitcoin tells us that the two assets move closely together. While this is unquestionably favourable when markets are rising, it comes at a cost when they are falling. In other words, Bitcoin offers less diversification away from traditional assets than some may expect. Consider a how a large sell-off in Bitcoin could trigger a forced liquidation of other assets, such as stocks. This could trigger a cascade of selling as leveraged positions are closed across-the-board, with Wall Street getting badly hurt and, in turn, weakening investor sentiment, so hitting Bitcoin again. It is worth noting that this potential spiral is hidden in the raw data.

Second, and as important, is the linkage between gold and Bitcoin. We have previously identified this relationship as a classic ‘error feedback’ mechanism that is common in statistical modelling and engineering. This operates between two separate variables: (1) the change in the gold price and (2) the ratio between BTC$ and gold.

Our models show an initial negative impact between gold and Bitcoin. In other words, a positive shock to gold prices causes a fall in Bitcoin prices around 4-to-6-weeks later. However, the second ratio term attenuates the response over time. This means that in the long-term gold and BTC$ are strongly positively correlated and trend, with BTC$ revealing a power law relationship with gold, i.e. as gold rises, BTC$ rises faster.

Some of the flavour of this relationship is captured in the following impulse response chart. It shows how the initial trend relationship between BTC$ and gold reverses into a negative correlation after a spike in gold and then progressively restores the positive linkage over time.

Expressed in more straightforward terms, gold and Bitcoin are often negatively correlated in the short-term, but show strong positive correlation over the long-term. This implied ‘catch-up’ effect tells investors to always watch the behaviour of the other asset, i.e. gold or Bitcoin. After a strong gold run, it might be wise to switch into Bitcoin. Similarly, vice versa.

In summary, we learn three things from this updated analysis:

(1) Global Liquidity is the dominant of three factors (liquidity, gold and investor sentiment) that systematically influence Bitcoin

(2) Gold and Bitcoin enjoy a symbiotic relationship: negative in the short-term, positive thereafter

(3) When these short-term effects wash-out over the long-term, a simple ‘rule of thumb’ says that roughly 50% of systematic influences on Bitcoin come from Global Liquidity; and 25% derive from each of gold and investor sentiment
